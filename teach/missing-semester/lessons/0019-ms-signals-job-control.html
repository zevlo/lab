Signals & job control

Ctrl-C, Ctrl-Z, fg/bg, jobs, and why closing a terminal kills background work — first slice of MS lecture 2.

Why this matters

You already kill and pgrep from the ops floor. Missing Semester’s Command-line Environment lecture adds the other half: the terminal sends signals, and the shell tracks jobs so you can pause, resume, and background without losing the prompt. That is how you free the keyboard mid-sleep, keep a long job running, and understand why a closed SSH window nukes orphaned children — and why tmux exists.

Read first (10–15 min): MS Command-line Environment — the “Signals” section only (through the sample sleep / nohup / jobs session). Skip Arguments, Streams, Remote Machines, and Customizing the Shell for now.

Knowledge 1 — signals from the keyboard

A signal is a software interrupt. The shell maps a few key chords to common ones:

KeySignalUsual effect
Ctrl-CSIGINTinterrupt — stop the foreground job
Ctrl-\SIGQUITquit — stronger stop (core dump on some systems)
Ctrl-ZSIGTSTPterminal stop — suspend the job
 
sleep 100
^C          # Ctrl-C → shell sends SIGINT → sleep dies
Processes can ignore SIGINT. Then Ctrl-C does nothing useful. Escalate: Ctrl-\, or kill -TERM pid, or last resort kill -KILL pid (SIGKILL cannot be caught — prefer TERM first).

Knowledge 2 — jobs, fg, bg, &

Ctrl-Z suspends. jobs lists unfinished jobs for this shell. Resume in the foreground with fg, or continue in the background with bg. Suffix & starts a command in the background immediately.

sleep 1000
^Z                    # suspended
jobs                  # [1]+  Suspended  sleep 1000
bg                    # continue in background
jobs                  # [1]+  Running    sleep 1000
fg                    # bring back to foreground (then Ctrl-C to stop)

sleep 60 &            # start already in background
jobs

Refer to a job as %1, %2, … (the number from jobs). That works with kill, fg, and bg:

kill %1
fg %1
bg %1
jobs is per-shell. A job started in another terminal (or another pane’s bash) will not show up here. Use pgrep/pkill across the machine; use jobs/% inside the shell that owns the job.

Knowledge 3 — SIGHUP, nohup, and why tmux

Close the terminal (or lose SSH) and children get SIGHUP (“hang up”) — most die. nohup cmd & ignores hangup and appends output to nohup.out. For interactive multi-pane work that should survive disconnects, you already know the durable answer: tmux (detach / reattach).

nohup sleep 2000 &
jobs
# closing this terminal would still kill a plain `sleep &`
# nohup resists SIGHUP; tmux keeps a full session alive
kill without a signal name sends SIGTERM. Same family as the graceful stop you used with pgrep/kill in Lesson 0008. kill -9 / SIGKILL is the blunt instrument — only when TERM fails.

Drill — suspend, background, find, kill

Ghostty + bash. One shell tab. Clean up leftover sleeps when done.

  1. Start a long sleep, suspend, continue in background: 
    sleep 10000
# Ctrl-Z
bg
jobs
    show answer

    You should see a suspended job after Ctrl-Z, then Running after bg. Note the job number in brackets — that is your %N.

  2. Find and kill it without typing the pid (MS exercise shape — you already know pgrep/pkill): 
    pgrep -lf sleep
pkill -f 'sleep 10000'
jobs
    show answer

    pgrep -lf lists pid + full command. pkill -f matches the command line. Prefer a specific pattern (sleep 10000) so you do not kill unrelated sleeps. jobs should be empty (or show exited).

  3. Background with &, then stop via job id: 
    sleep 30 &
jobs
kill %1
jobs
    show answer

    & returns the prompt immediately. kill %1 sends SIGTERM to that job. Confirm with jobs.

  4. Feel nohup (optional — skip if short on time): 
    nohup sleep 20 &
jobs
# leave it; or: kill %1
ls -l nohup.out 2>/dev/null || true
    show answer

    nohup prints a note about appending to nohup.out. The job still appears under jobs while it runs.

  5. Cold rebuilds (no peeking):
    1. Suspend a foreground sleep, continue it in the background, list jobs.
    2. Kill the latest job with %N (not a raw pid).
    3. One sentence: what signal does closing the terminal send, and what tool keeps a session alive instead of nohup?
    show answer

    (a) sleep 10000 → Ctrl-Z → bg → jobs
    (b) kill %1 (use the number jobs showed)
    (c) SIGHUP; durable interactive answer is tmux (detach/reattach).

Check your understanding

Four questions. Shuffled answers.

Q1What does Ctrl-C usually send?

  • SIGINT (interrupt)
  • SIGHUP (hang up)
  • SIGKILL (force die)
  • SIGTSTP (suspend)

Q2After Ctrl-Z, how do you keep running in background?

  • type bg
  • type fg again
  • Ctrl-C then &
  • only kill -9 works

Q3What is %1 in kill %1?

  • job number from jobs
  • always pid 1 (init)
  • first awk field
  • script argument $1

Q4Closing the terminal typically sends children…?

  • SIGHUP
  • SIGINT only
  • SIGTSTP
  • no signal at all
