Your GPU and the VRAM Budget

The one mental model the entire inference stack is built on.

Why this, first? You're building deep mastery of the inference stack — no rush. But there's one idea that everything else hangs from, so we plant it on day one: every advanced topic you'll study (quantization, batching, KV caching, vLLM, autoscaling) is ultimately a strategy for spending one scarce resource — GPU memory. We'll make it concrete on your actual RTX A2000 6 GB, using small models, so the budget is real and tight enough to feel.

1. The one idea

A CPU runs your code against system RAM. A GPU runs its kernels against its own memory, called VRAM. The iron rule of inference:

Everything the model needs to compute must fit in VRAM at the same time — and VRAM is small and fixed. VRAM = weights + KV cache + activations/overhead

If it fits, the model runs. If it doesn't, you get a CUDA out-of-memory error and nothing runs.4 Almost every inference-engineering technique exists to make more useful work fit inside that fixed number. So step one is simply: know your number.

  • Confirm the total VRAM reads ~6144 MiB (6 GB).

Tip: nvidia-smi --query-gpu=name,memory.total,memory.used,memory.free --format=csv prints just the numbers, script-friendly.

3. Where the budget goes

Three tenants share your 6 GB:

① Weights — the parameters, always resident. Memory is straightforward:

weights (bytes) ≈ num_params × bytes_per_param

A 3.8B model (Phi-4-mini) at FP16 (2 bytes each) is 3.8e9 × 2 ≈ 7.6 GB — already too big for your 6 GB card. That single fact is why quantization exists: store each weight in ~½ byte (INT4) and the same model drops to ~1.9 GB and fits with room to spare.5

② KV cache — per-token memory of the conversation so far, so the model never recomputes past tokens. It grows with context length and batch size and competes with weights for space. For a 13B model, one token of cache is ~800 KB; a full 2048-token request can be ~1.6 GB.3 On 6 GB, this is exactly why we pair small weights with sane context limits — a whole lesson on its own later.

③ Activations + overhead — temporary tensors during the forward pass, plus the CUDA/PyTorch context. You must leave headroom; serving engines reserve a fraction by default (vLLM's gpu_memory_utilization defaults to 0.9 — grab 90%, leave 10%).6

5. The hidden ceiling: bandwidth, not compute

Here's the insight that separates people who've run models from people who've only read about them. When generating text one token at a time (the decode phase), the GPU must stream every weight out of VRAM for each token. So your single-stream token rate is capped by memory bandwidth, not raw compute:5

max tokens/sec (single stream) ≈ memory_bandwidth ÷ model_bytes

Your A2000 moves 288 GB/s. A 2.5 GB (Q4) model has a ceiling around 288 ÷ 2.5 ≈ 115 tok/s — but that's an optimistic upper bound. Measured rates on small cards land lower (~20–30 tok/s) because attention, sampling, and kernel launches add cost and you never hit 100% of peak bandwidth.7 The calculator shows the ceiling so you can reason about the gap. This is also why batching matters: serving many requests at once reuses each expensive weight-read, multiplying total throughput without more bandwidth.

Go deeper (you asked for fundamentals): MiB vs MB matters here — nvidia-smi reports mebibytes (1 MiB = 1024² bytes), while datasheets quote GB in powers of ten. And "288 GB/s" itself comes from memory clock × bus width (the A2000's 192-bit bus × GDDR6 speed). We'll unpack the memory hierarchy properly in Lesson 04.
