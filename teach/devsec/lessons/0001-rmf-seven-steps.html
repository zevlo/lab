DevSec Engineering · Lesson 0001

The Risk Management Framework: Seven Steps

Why this lesson. Your target postings require “DoD requirements and processes including Risk Management Framework requirements within classified environments.” Every classified system at Lockheed Martin lives inside this framework. The interview will ask you to walk through it.

One win. By the end you can name all seven steps in order, name the four key documents, and say where a DevSecOps engineer plugs in — without notes.

The idea in one breath

A system is any organized information technology that processes information for a mission. A network, a server, and a pipeline can each be a system. The Risk Management Framework (RMF) is a process that manages security risk — the chance that a threat harms the system and the mission. The RMF runs across the whole life cycle of a system. It is a cycle, not a checklist. A system enters it. A system stays in it.

The RMF has seven steps. NIST defines the RMF in SP 800-37 Rev. 2. The Department of Defense adopts it in DoDI 8510.01, which you can reach through the DoD Cyber Exchange RMF portal.

The seven steps

1

Prepare

The organization gets ready before any system work starts. It identifies common controls it will provide once, for many systems. It sets rules for tailoring controls later. Preparation makes every later step cheaper.

Your seat: Learn the system boundary, the data types, and the approved tool list. Feed compliance targets into the team backlog now, not after the audit.

2

Categorize

The team rates the system by impact. Impact means: what happens to the mission if the system fails? FIPS 199 defines the yardstick. Each property — confidentiality, integrity, availability — gets a rating: low, moderate, or high. The highest rating wins and becomes the category of the system.

A weapons-delivery system is high. A public website is low. Most DoD systems land at moderate or high.

Your seat: The category sets the rigor of everything downstream. More rigor means more controls, more evidence, slower changes. Know the category before you design.

3

Select

The team picks the controls. A security control is one safeguard or countermeasure — for example “enforce account lockout.” A control baseline is the starting list of controls for a category. SP 800-53B publishes the baselines: low, moderate, high. The team tailors the baseline. It removes controls that do not apply. It adds controls for new threats. The chosen controls are written into the System Security Plan (SysSP).

Your seat: Map controls to pipeline capability early. Know which control families will hit you: access control (AC), audit (AU), configuration management (CM), system integrity (SI).

4

Implement

The team deploys the selected controls and documents them in the SysSP. In the Department of Defense, DISA publishes STIGs — Security Technical Implementation Guides. A STIG is the concrete how-to for a technology: the exact settings that satisfy the controls. Lesson 0002 covers STIGs.

Your seat: This step is most of your engineering work. STIG hardening, secrets in Vault, image scanning — you build the controls.

5

Assess

An independent assessor — a person outside the build team — tests whether the controls work. The assessor follows the procedures in SP 800-53A. The assessor writes a Security Assessment Plan (SAP) first: what will be tested, and how. The assessor writes a Security Assessment Report (SAR) after: what passed, what failed.

Your seat: Automate the evidence. SCAP scans, audit logs, pipeline artifacts. An assessor with clean evidence finishes faster and finds fewer surprises.

6

Authorize

The authorizing official (AO) — a senior official, not the build team — reads the SAR. The AO also reads the Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M): the list of gaps, each with an owner and a date. The AO decides: is the remaining risk acceptable? If yes, the AO issues an authorization to operate (ATO). If no, the AO denies it.

An ATO is not a safety certificate. An ATO is a decision to accept residual risk.

Your seat: The ATO gates go-live. Your scan results and honest POA&M entries are what the AO reads.

7

Monitor

The system operates. The team keeps watching. SP 800-137 defines information security continuous monitoring. The team scans for drift, assesses changes, tracks new vulnerabilities, and burns down the POA&M. Monitoring feeds ongoing authorization: the AO re-accepts the risk on evidence, not on a three-year redo.

Your seat: Drift detection, scheduled STIG re-scans, patch pipelines. This is DevSecOps earning the “Sec” — continuous compliance instead of annual paperwork.

The four documents

DocumentAnswersWritten by
SysSP — System Security PlanWhich controls are chosen, and how each is implementedThe build team, maintained over time
SAP — Security Assessment PlanHow the assessment will be runThe assessor, before the assessment
SAR — Security Assessment ReportWhat the assessment foundThe assessor, after the assessment
POA&M — Plan of Action and MilestonesWhich gaps remain, who fixes each, by whenThe build team, reviewed by the AO

The DoD overlay

NIST writes the RMF. The Department of Defense adds its own layer:

ItemWhat it is
DoDI 8510.01The DoD instruction that adopts the NIST RMF for DoD systems (access via DoD Cyber Exchange)
eMASSDoD's system of record. Authorization packages live and move inside it
CNSSI 1253Enhancements to categorization and baselines for national security systems
STIGsDISA's implementation guides. The concrete settings that satisfy controls (cyber.mil/stigs)
ReciprocityOne authorization accepted across organizations, to avoid duplicating assessments

Say it in an interview

RMF is a life-cycle process: prepare, categorize by impact, select a control baseline, implement, assess independently, authorize by risk decision, then monitor continuously.
An ATO is not a safety certificate. It is a senior official's decision to accept residual risk.
In DoD, DISA STIGs make the 800-53 controls concrete, eMASS holds the authorization package, and my job as a DevSecOps engineer is to automate the evidence.
