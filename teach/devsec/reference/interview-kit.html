Interview Kit

Reference — how to structure a behavioral answer, a story bank mapped to the job's skill areas, and model technical answers. Each lesson adds to it.

Key message. The employer's interview guide (linked in the workspace RESOURCES.md) says most hiring managers use the STAR method. Expect a panel. Much of the technical depth will come out through questions about your past work. So prepare stories first, and practice saying the technical answers out loud.

STAR: how to structure a story

A behavioral question asks for a real example: "Tell me about a time when…". Answer in four parts. Spend most of the time on what you did. [MIT CAPD]

PartShareWhat to say
Situation20%The context, in two or three sentences. The panel does not need every detail.
Task10%What you were responsible for, or the goal.
Action60%The steps you took. Name the tools, the choices, and why you made them.
Result10%What changed, with a number if you have one. Add what you learned.
  • Say "I", not "we". In a team story, describe your own part.
  • Give specific examples, not general claims.
  • Tell the truth. Invented details do not survive follow-up questions.
  • Prepare 3–5 stories as bullet outlines, not scripts. A memorized script is hard to adapt and can sound unnatural.
  • One story can answer several questions. Practice adapting it on the spot.

Your story bank

Write your drafts in ~/devsec-lab/star-stories.md. Keep that file out of any Git repository, because it describes real work. Copy this template once for each story:

## Story: <short title>
Skill areas: <one or two from the table below>

S (20%): Where and when? What was at stake?
T (10%): What were you responsible for?
A (60%): What did you do, step by step? Which tools? Which choices, and why?
R (10%): What changed? Use a number: time saved, failures avoided, findings fixed.
Learned: <one line>

Technical answer bank

Each entry is a skeleton, not a script. Say it out loud in your own words, in about one to two minutes. The last line is a one-sentence version for when time is short.

From lesson 0001: security gates

"How would you add security to a CI/CD pipeline?"

  1. Add controls in order of risk reduction: secret scanning, then dependency scanning (SCA), then branch protection with SAST. This is the OWASP order.
  2. Run each control where someone can act on it: pre-commit and merge request checks first, image scanning and signing at build, admission policy at deploy, and rescans in production.
  3. Make each control a gate: it exits nonzero on findings above a threshold, and a passing pipeline is required to merge.
  4. Gate on risk, not counts: fail on new HIGH or CRITICAL findings that have a fix. Baseline the rest, and allow documented exceptions.
  5. Protect the pipeline itself: short-lived credentials, pinned images, least privilege, and verified tools.

One line: "I add controls in order of risk reduction, make each one a gate that fails on new high-risk findings, and harden the pipeline itself."

"A developer pushed an API key, then deleted it in the next commit. What do you do?"

  1. Treat the key as exposed. Revoke it and issue a new one first. Deleting it from the code does not help: the old commit still holds it, and copies of the repository may exist.
  2. Check the provider's logs for any use of the old key between the push and the revocation.
  3. Scan the full history, for example with gitleaks git, to find any other leaks.
  4. Decide about history. Rewriting it disrupts everyone and does not reach existing clones. After rotation, a recorded exception is often enough.
  5. Prevent a repeat: a pre-commit hook plus a CI gate, and move the value into CI variables or a secrets manager.

One line: "Rotate first, check for use, scan the history, then close the gap that let it in."

"The scanner reports 200 findings. What should fail the build?"

  1. Not the count. Fail on new findings that are HIGH or CRITICAL and have a fix available.
  2. Baseline the existing findings, and give them due dates by severity.
  3. Rank with context: is it being exploited, is it likely to be, and is the code reachable and exposed? Lesson 0004 covers this.
  4. Allow documented exceptions with a reason and an owner, so no one turns the gate off.
  5. Report the trend: new findings blocked, backlog size, and time to fix.

One line: "Block new, high-risk, fixable findings; baseline and schedule the rest."
