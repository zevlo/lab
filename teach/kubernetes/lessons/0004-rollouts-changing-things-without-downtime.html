Lesson 0004 · Kubernetes Fundamentals

Rollouts: Changing Things Without Downtime

The control loop, applied to change itself.

Everything so far kept a steady desired state running. But the most dangerous moment in operations isn't steady state — it's the deploy. At work, this lesson is the difference between "we shipped at 2pm" and "we were down at 2pm." In the homelab, it's how you upgrade a service without your apps going dark. The good news: you already know the mechanism. A rollout is just the control loop chasing a moving target.

The Idea

Recall from lesson 0001: a Deployment doesn't manage pods directly — it manages a ReplicaSet, which manages pods. That extra layer looked like bureaucracy. Rollouts are why it exists.

When you change a Deployment's pod template (the image, an env var — anything inside spec.template), the Deployment controller doesn't touch the existing pods. It creates a second ReplicaSet for the new template and starts shifting replicas: new RS 0→1→2→3, old RS 3→2→1→0 [docs]. Your Service, selecting by label, sends traffic to whichever pods are Ready — old and new alike — so there's never a gap.

The default strategy, RollingUpdate, has two knobs that bound the shuffle [docs]:

  • maxSurge — how many pods above the desired count may exist during the update (default 25%)
  • maxUnavailable — how many may be missing (default 25%)

And because the old ReplicaSet isn't deleted — just scaled to zero — it becomes a revision: a checkpoint. kubectl rollout undo isn't magic; it scales the old ReplicaSet back up and the new one down. Same machinery, run in reverse [docs].

The mental model in one line: a rollout is two ReplicaSets and a controller sliding replicas from one to the other — and a rollback is the same slide, backwards.

Lab — Ship It, Break It, Roll It Back (~20 min)

Against OrbStack, two terminals.

1

Start with a known-good deployment, and note the ReplicaSet behind it:

kubectl config use-context orbstack
kubectl create deployment web --image=nginx:1.27 --replicas=3
kubectl get replicasets

Expect: one ReplicaSet, web-<hash>, with 3/3 ready. The hash is computed from the pod template — remember that.

2

In terminal 2, watch ReplicaSets — this is the best seat in the house for a rollout:

kubectl get replicasets -w
3

In terminal 1, ship a new version — change the image:

kubectl set image deployment/web nginx=nginx:1.29
kubectl rollout status deployment/web

Watch terminal 2: a second ReplicaSet appears (different hash — different template), and the counts slide: new one climbs to 3, old one drains to 0. rollout status narrates the same story. Note the old ReplicaSet is still listed — at 0. That's your checkpoint.

4

Look at the paper trail:

kubectl rollout history deployment/web
kubectl get replicasets

Expect: two revisions in the history, two ReplicaSets — one at 3, one at 0. Each revision is a ReplicaSet.

5

Diagnose and fix. Now ship a bad deploy — a typo'd image tag that doesn't exist:

kubectl set image deployment/web nginx=nginx:1.29-tpyo
kubectl rollout status deployment/web

rollout status hangs — the rollout is stuck. Ctrl-C it. Before reading on, diagnose it yourself; you know the tools (get pods, describe). Then compare with:

kubectl get pods
kubectl describe pod <the-broken-pod>   # read the Events at the bottom

Expect: one new pod stuck in ErrImagePull / ImagePullBackOff, and Events saying the image can't be pulled. Now the important part — check what the rollout did to your working pods:

kubectl get pods   # count the Running ones

Three old pods are still Running and serving. The rollout halted with the bad version contained, because maxUnavailable wouldn't let the controller tear down healthy pods before replacements were Ready. A failed deploy did not become an outage. This is the rolling-update guarantee, seen from the wreckage.

6

Roll it back — and watch terminal 2 while you do:

kubectl rollout undo deployment/web
kubectl rollout status deployment/web
kubectl rollout history deployment/web

Expect: the stuck ReplicaSet drains to 0, the previous one is already at 3 — instant recovery, because the good pods never left. The history shows the rolled-back revision moved to the top with a new number. Nothing was deleted; replicas just slid back.

7

Clean up:

kubectl delete deployment web
Caveat for real life: the containment in step 5 worked because the failure was loud (image won't even pull). A new version that starts but is broken will happily receive traffic — guarding against that requires readiness probes, a future lesson. Don't leave today thinking rollouts catch every bad deploy.
