Glossary

CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 · Plain-language definitions · Grows every lesson — last updated after Lesson 0014

A · B · C · D · E · F · H · I · J · K · L · M · N · O · P · R · S · T · V · W · Z

A

Accounting (the third A in AAA)
Recording what each identity actually did, so actions can be reviewed later. Login events in a central log; an audit trail.
Adaptive identity
Zero trust idea: authentication strength adapts to risk. A login from a new country triggers a stronger challenge.
Advanced persistent threat (APT)
An operating pattern, usually nation-state or state-adjacent: advanced tools, persistent access maintained quietly for months (dwell time), and a funded actor behind it. Not a piece of malware — a patient adversary.
Application allow list
Only approved applications may run — execution is default-deny. The exam's strongest answer against malware installation and unauthorized tooling. A kiosk locked to two vendor-approved apps.
Asymmetric encryption
Encryption with a key pair: a public key shared with the world and a private key that never leaves its owner. Public encrypts → private decrypts (confidentiality); private signs → public verifies (signatures). RSA, ECC.
Asset inventory
The authoritative list of every asset — owner, location, classification, lifecycle state — kept true by tracking and discovery. You cannot protect what you cannot name; the undocumented asset is unprotected by definition.
Attack surface
The sum of every point an attacker could reach and try — open ports, applications, devices, removable media, and people. Reduced, never eliminated: close ports, retire legacy systems, train staff.
Attack vector
The route an attack travels to its target: a phishing email, a poisoned update, a dropped flash drive. Simple test: the vector is how, the surface is where.
Authentication (the first A in AAA)
Proving who you are, with evidence such as a password, a token, or a fingerprint. Password plus one-time code — multifactor authentication (MFA).
Authorization (the second A in AAA)
Deciding what an authenticated identity is allowed to do. A finance role that permits opening the payroll folder.

B

Backup (full / incremental / differential)
Three copy strategies. A full copies everything. An incremental copies changes since the last backup of any kind — restoring needs the full plus every incremental, in order. A differential copies changes since the last full — restoring needs exactly two pieces, always. Incrementals back up fastest; differentials restore simplest.
Baseline configuration
The documented known-good state of a system — every setting, patch, and service. Deploy from it, measure against it, re-apply it when drift appears. A CIS benchmark or DoD STIG rendered in code.
Beaconing
An infected host calling its command-and-control server on a fixed, timed schedule — low volume, regular as clockwork. The traffic fingerprint of malware phoning home. A callout every 60 seconds, night and day.
Bloatware
Preinstalled, unwanted software. Less an attack than an attack surface — vendor utilities nobody uses and nobody patches.
Blockchain
An append-only ledger copied across many machines, where each block carries the hash of the previous block. Tampering with an old block breaks every hash after it — tamper-evident by construction.
Boot attestation
A central verifier reviews a machine's measured-boot record before granting access. Detects a tampered boot chain remotely; it does not block one.
Botnet (zombie army)
A fleet of compromised machines remotely controlled in unison — rented out for denial-of-service, spam, or mining. Each member machine is a zombie.
Buffer overflow
Input exceeds the memory allocated to hold it, overwriting adjacent memory — crash, or attacker-chosen code execution. The natural habitat is old C code; the fix is patching and memory-safe languages.
Bug bounty
A formal vendor program that pays for verified vulnerability reports — responsible disclosure with a paycheck. Turns the world's researchers into an extended security team.
BYOD / COPE / corporate-owned
Three ownership models for phones. BYOD (bring your own device): the employee's phone, work confined to a managed container. COPE (corporate-owned, personally enabled): the company's phone, personal use allowed. Corporate-owned: the company's phone, full stop — the defense default, maximum wipe rights.
Business email compromise (BEC)
Impersonating an executive or vendor over email — often from a look-alike domain — to redirect a payment. The message vector with the authority and urgency levers pulled at once.

C

Capacity planning
Sizing people, technology, and infrastructure for peak demand, not average. Spare capacity is also what absorbs a failure — it runs with resiliency.
Captive portal
A web page that asks who you are before granting network access. Acceptance, not authentication — fine for guest Wi-Fi, useless where identity must be proven.
Certificate (digital)
A document binding a public key to an identity, signed by a certificate authority. The unit of trust in PKI.
Certificate authority (CA)
The trusted issuer that verifies identity and signs certificates. A root CA's certificate is pre-installed in browsers and operating systems — trusted because it is already there.
Certificate of destruction
The disposal vendor's signed, itemized record that named media were destroyed. Proof, not protection — a managerial control closing the audit chain from sanitization to disposal.
Certificate signing request (CSR)
The application for a certificate: your public key plus identity details, sent to a CA for signing.
Chain of trust
Root CA signs intermediate CAs; intermediates sign leaf certificates. Verification walks from the presented leaf up to a root the verifier already trusts.
Change advisory board (CAB)
A cross-team group that reviews proposed changes for risk and impact, and approves or rejects them. The checkpoint between "proposed" and "scheduled."
Change management
The documented process controlling every modification to a system: request, risk and impact analysis, approval, testing, a scheduled window, a tested rollback plan, and records. Exists because uncontrolled change is a leading cause of outages and security regressions.
Change request
The formal proposal for a modification: what will change, why, the risks, and the plan to back it out.
CIA triad
The three goals of security: confidentiality, integrity, availability. Every attack harms at least one goal; every control defends at least one.
Cloud service models (IaaS / PaaS / SaaS)
The responsibility ladder. IaaS: you run the OS and up. PaaS: you run applications only. SaaS: the provider runs everything; you configure. The higher the service, the less you patch.
Cloud deployment models (public / private / hybrid / community)
Public: anyone who pays. Private: one organization. Hybrid: private plus public, connected. Community: organizations with common concerns — compliance, clearance, regulation — sharing dedicated infrastructure.
Clustering
Multiple servers acting as one service; a node dies, the others keep answering. The server-room form of no single point of failure.
Code signing
The developer signs the binary with a private key; the operating system verifies the signature before running it. Tampered or substituted software fails the check — the fix for malicious updates.
Cold site
A recovery site with space, power, and cooling but no systems — bring everything. Days to weeks to operate. The cheapest site type; cost runs inverse to recovery time.
Compensating control (type)
A substitute control used when the ideal control is not possible. An encrypted VPN instead of an expensive private circuit.
Confidentiality
Only approved people can read the data. Encryption and access controls defend it; a stolen customer list harms it.
Containerization (mobile)
Work apps and work data sealed in their own sandbox on a personal phone. The MAM enforcement line: the container is managed and remotely erasable; the rest of the phone is not.
Control plane
The zero trust brain: where access decisions are made. Users never touch it.
Corrective control (type)
A control that repairs damage after an incident. Restoring from backup; applying an emergency patch; a rollback plan.
Credentialed vs. non-credentialed scan
Logged in versus locked out. A credentialed scan reads installed versions and configurations directly — deeper, more accurate. Non-credentialed sees the attacker's outside view — shallower, more false positives. The exam's tell: the scanner authenticating to hosts.
Certificate revocation list (CRL)
The CA's published list of revoked certificate serial numbers. Can be large and goes stale between downloads.
Cryptojacking
Unauthorized cryptocurrency mining on someone else's hardware. Signature indicator: sustained resource consumption against baseline.
Cross-site scripting (XSS)
Untrusted input becomes part of a web page shown to other users, running attacker script in their browsers. Client-side: the visitors are the victims. Fixed by output encoding — render user content as text, never markup.
CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures)
The public catalog of named, disclosed vulnerabilities — CVE-YYYY-number. The name: everyone discussing "that TLS flaw" means the same entry. Where the name gets its number is NVD, the National Vulnerability Database.
CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System)
The standardized 0–10 severity score for a vulnerability — attack vector, complexity, privileges required, impact. How bad the flaw could be; 9.0+ is critical. Says nothing about your network: exposure and asset value do the rest.

D

Data classification
Labels that rank data by the damage disclosure would cause. Government: top secret, secret, confidential, unclassified. Commercial: restricted, confidential, internal, public. The exam asks which label fits which data.
Data masking
Showing only part of a real value. Support staff see "**** **** **** 1234."
Data minimization
Collecting only the data needed and keeping it only as long as needed. The cheapest protection is data never held.
Data plane
The zero trust traffic: where users and applications actually interact, after the control plane says yes.
Data retention
The policy for how long data is kept, with secure destruction at the end of the lifetime. Data kept past its purpose is stored risk.
Data sovereignty
Data is subject to the laws of the country where it physically resides — the reason a defense contract can block the cheap foreign cloud region. Enforced through geolocation restrictions.
Data states
At rest: on storage — protect with encryption. In transit: crossing the network — protect with TLS or a VPN. In use: in memory, being processed — protect with secure enclaves. The exam's tell: where is the data at the moment the question describes?
Deauthentication attack
Forcing wireless clients off the network — often to capture the reconnect handshake, or to herd victims toward an evil twin.
Degausser
A magnetic field strong enough to erase a drive completely — data, servo tracks, firmware. Magnetic media only (hard disks, tape); useless on solid-state drives; the drive is dead afterward. Purging by execution.
Denial of service (DoS / DDoS)
Flooding or exhausting a service until it cannot answer. Distributed (DDoS) = the flood comes from many machines at once, usually a rented botnet. Availability attacks by definition.
Deterrent control (type)
A control that discourages an attempt before it starts. It works on motivation, not mechanics. A warning sign; a visible camera; a guard dog.
Detective control (type)
A control that discovers an attack or a policy violation, while it happens or after. An intrusion detection system (IDS) alert; closed-circuit television (CCTV) footage; a log review.
Digital signature
A hash of the content, encrypted with the signer's private key. Proves integrity, authenticity, and non-repudiation — not confidentiality. Anyone verifies it with the signer's public key.
Directive control (type)
A control that tells people what to do. It steers behavior rather than blocking it. An acceptable use policy (AUP); a patching procedure; a change management policy.
Directory traversal
Input paths like ../../etc/passwd walk out of the web root to read files that should be unreachable. The path, not the payload, is the attack.
DNS poisoning
Corrupting a resolver's cache so a trusted name resolves to the attacker's address — an integrity attack on the name-to-address mapping.
DNS sinkhole
A disruption technique: Domain Name System (DNS) answers for known-malicious domains point to a dead end, breaking malware's command-and-control (C2) callbacks.
Domain hijacking
Taking over the registration of a domain itself — all its traffic and mail redirect.
Downgrade attack
Tricking a handshake into negotiating older, weaker cryptography, then breaking the weak version. "The connection settled on TLS 1.0."
Dwell time
The stretch of time an attacker stays present and unnoticed in a compromised environment. Months of quiet dwell time is the APT's signature.
Dynamic code analysis (DAST)
Testing a running application — feed it crafted inputs, watch what breaks. Catches what source-reading misses: configuration, environment, runtime behavior. Needs a live system; that is the dividing line with SAST.

E

Elasticity
Capacity that grows and shrinks on demand. The dividing line with scalability: scalability is one-directional growth; elasticity is bidirectional. Servers added before the sale, removed after.
End-of-life (EOL)
The vendor has ended support — no patches will ever come. The answer is replace or isolate; in DoD environments, running EOL software fails inspections. Distinguish from zero-day: EOL will never be patched; a zero-day will be, eventually.
Espionage
Motivation: stealing secrets — government or corporate — for a state or competitor's benefit. Slow exfiltration of design documents over months.
Evil twin
A rogue access point broadcasting a legitimate SSID — auto-joining laptops hand it their traffic. The fake place to connect; deauthentication is the shove that drives victims there.

F

Fail-open vs. fail-close
What a dead inline security device does with traffic. Fail-open passes everything — availability first. Fail-close drops everything — security first. Defense networks choose close. An IPS that loses power.
False positive
Flagged, but not real — the scanner guessed wrong. Wastes effort until validated; the cure is validation before remediation. Its mirror, the false negative, is the dangerous one: a real weakness never flagged.
Fileless malware
Malware that runs entirely in memory, inside legitimate processes — nothing on disk to scan. Enabled by memory injection; clears on reboot, returns on re-infection.
Forward proxy
Intermediary for internal users going out — filters, caches, hides the clients. Direction decides the name: serves the users, faces the internet.
Full device encryption
Everything on the device is ciphertext at rest, keyed to the unlock. The lost-phone answer for reading the data; remote wipe is the answer for removing it.
Fuzzing
Feeding a program random and malformed input — mutated copies of known-bad shapes — while watching for crashes. Automated chaos that finds what test suites never imagined: the input family's cousins.

G

Geofencing
Policy tied to location — a capability granted or denied based on where the device physically is. The camera works everywhere except inside the building.
Geolocation restriction
Data may live only in approved physical locations. The enforcement mechanism under data sovereignty.
Geographic dispersal
Keeping copies far enough apart that one disaster cannot reach both. Primary in one region, replica in another.
Generator
Fuel-driven power for hours to days. The uninterruptible power supply holds the minutes; the generator holds the day.

H

Hacktivist
An attacker driven by ideology: defacement, leaks, and disruption as protest. Skill varies; the cause is on display. Defacing a retailer's site over labor practices, demanding no money.
Hardened image
A baseline baked into a ready-to-deploy template — patched, minimal, locked down. Every new machine starts hardened, never default. A golden virtual machine image built in CI.
Hash (hashing)
A one-way, fixed-length fingerprint of any input. Same input, same fingerprint; one changed character, completely different fingerprint; cannot be reversed. The integrity tool. SHA-256.
Heat map
The site survey's output: signal strength drawn over the floor plan. Coverage, interference, and channel overlap made visible.
Honeyfile
A decoy document that trips an alert when opened. A planted "salaries.xlsx."
Honeynet
A decoy network of honeypots.
Honeypot
A decoy system built to look valuable, recording everything an attacker does on it.
Hot site
A running replica of production with data synced — back in minutes. The most expensive site type; cost runs inverse to recovery time.
Hypervisor (type 1 / type 2)
Type 1 runs directly on server hardware — bare metal, the enterprise default. Type 2 runs as an application inside a host operating system — one more layer, more attack surface. A laptop virtualizer is type 2.

I

IEEE 802.1X
Port-level authentication: the switch forwards nothing until the connecting device proves its identity via EAP. Identity before connectivity — zero trust applied to the physical jack.
Impersonation
Posing as a person or brand the target trusts — a recruiter, a bank, a vendor. Works with a pretext (the story) and against the familiarity lever.
Information sharing and analysis center (ISAC)
Sector peers — finance, health, government, defense — sharing incident signatures and attacker tradecraft under trusted-sharing rules. Real attacks, seen by real victims, hours old. Distinct from a bug bounty: victims sharing what happened, not vendors paying for flaws.
Immutable backup
A copy that cannot be changed or deleted for a set period — write once, read many (WORM), object lock. Ransomware holding admin credentials still cannot touch it.
Immutable systems
Systems never modified after deployment — replaced from a clean build instead. Nothing persists to infect. Container images rebuilt in CI, never edited in production.
Impossible travel
Indicator: one account logging in from two places no human could cover in the time between — Virginia, then Tokyo, forty minutes later. Suggests credential compromise.
Indicators of compromise (IoC)
Observable departures from baseline that suggest malicious activity: account lockouts, concurrent sessions, impossible travel, blocked content, resource consumption, out-of-cycle logging, missing logs. The objective-2.4 skill: name what the observation suggests.
Industrial control system (ICS) / SCADA
Systems supervising physical processes — grid, water, factories. Availability outranks confidentiality, controllers often cannot be patched, and isolation is the exam's paired answer.
Input validation
Checking data against strict expectations — an allow list of permitted values — before using it. The root cure for the input family; for SQL injection, parameterized queries are the stronger, by-construction fix.
Implicit trust zone
The anti-pattern zero trust exists to eliminate: an area where being inside implies trust. "It's on the internal VLAN, so it must be safe."
Infrastructure as code (IaC)
Infrastructure defined, versioned, and applied from templates — configuration enforcement's native form. Drift becomes visible, and the fix is a re-apply, not a hand edit.
Insider threat
An attacker or risk source already inside authentication. Malicious: legitimate access misused with intent — revenge, espionage, sabotage. Negligent: no intent, just error — phishing clicks, misdirected files, lost devices.
Intrusion detection system (IDS) / intrusion prevention system (IPS)
Same engine, different seat. IDS receives a copy on a span port — detects, alerts, cannot block (detective). IPS sits inline — blocks in the path (preventive) and must choose fail-open or fail-close.
Integrity
Data stays correct and complete, with nothing altered or added. Hashing defends it; an attacker changing a transfer amount in transit harms it — so does an undocumented change to a production system.

J

Isolation
Removing a system from the network entirely — an air gap or a sandbox. The strongest boundary, and the least convenient. The answer for unpatchable crown jewels.
Jailbreaking
Stripping the vendor's operating-system controls on Apple devices to run unauthorized code. The Android sibling is rooting.

K

Jump server (bastion host)
One hardened, fully audited host through which all administrative access passes — so every admin action lands in a log. The people-path; a proxy is the data-path.
Keylogger
Records keystrokes to capture credentials as they are typed. The specific tool; spyware is the broader category.
Key management
The full life of cryptographic keys: generate, exchange, store (hardware security module, TPM), rotate, revoke, destroy. The keys are small; their handling is the security.

L

Load balancer
Distributes traffic across a server pool, with health checks that pull dead members out of rotation. Availability and scale in one appliance.
Logic bomb
Dormant code that detonates when a trigger condition fires — a date, an event, a name leaving payroll. The signature malware of the malicious insider.

M

Maintenance window
A pre-agreed, low-usage time slot in which changes are implemented, so disruption harms the fewest users. Protects availability.
Media sanitization (erase / clear / purge / destroy)
The four-rung ladder. Erase: pointers removed, data remains. Clear: every sector overwritten — recoverable only in a laboratory, fit for in-org reuse. Purge: degauss or crypto-erase — effectively unrecoverable, fit for leaving custody. Destroy: shred, incinerate, pulverize — the only rung for classified data. Classification picks the rung; destination confirms it.
Mean time between failures (MTBF)
Average time a component runs before it fails. Measures the equipment's reliability — set by purchasing and design, not by operations.
Mean time to repair (MTTR)
Average time to fix a failed component and restore service. Measures operational readiness — spares, staff, runbooks.
Measured boot
The trusted platform module (TPM) records a fingerprint of every boot step. The record makes boot tampering visible — detection, not blocking.
Microsegmentation
Segmentation pushed to the individual workload: each workload reaches only its own dependencies. Zero trust's threat scope reduction, implemented in software.
Misconfiguration
The self-inflicted vulnerability category: open storage buckets, default credentials, permissive rules. The most common breach source — and the exam's answer to "most common."
Memory injection (DLL / process injection)
Pushing malicious code into a running process's memory so it wears a legitimate process's name. Nothing is written to disk — the enabler of fileless malware.
Mobile device management (MDM / MAM / UEM)
Three management scopes. Mobile device management (MDM): the whole device. Mobile application management (MAM): work applications only — the BYOD fit. Unified endpoint management (UEM): every endpoint from one console.
Mobile site
A recovery site on a trailer, towed to where it is needed. Days to operate; situational cost.

N

Nation-state actor
The best-funded, most sophisticated attacker class: patient, custom tools, targets infrastructure and secrets. Motivations: espionage, warfare, disruption.
Next-generation firewall (NGFW)
Firewall with intelligence: application-aware, identity-aware, deep packet inspection with IPS built in. The dividing line with a UTM — intelligence versus bundling.
Non-persistence
Any change vanishes on reboot; the machine returns to its baseline. Malware that modifies the system does not survive. Lab virtual machines that revert to snapshot.
Non-repudiation
Proof of an action so strong that the actor cannot deny it. A digital signature on a contract.

O

OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol)
The client asks the CA's responder in real time whether a certificate is still valid. Fresh but slower, and every visit leaks a query to the CA.
OCSP stapling
The server fetches its own fresh OCSP proof and staples it to the handshake. The exam's answer whenever a scenario says the server presents proof of valid status.
Obfuscation
Hiding or substituting data instead of encrypting it — steganography, tokenization, masking.
On-path attack
Positioned between client and server, reading and altering both directions (formerly man-in-the-middle). Needs no malware on either endpoint — position is the whole attack.
Organized crime actor
Professional crews whose motive is profit: ransomware, phishing, fraud — run like a business, down to payment support portals.
OSINT (open-source intelligence)
Intelligence gathered from everything public: registries, job posts, DNS, social media, leak dumps. Free and legal — for defenders and attackers alike. In a pentest it is passive reconnaissance: learning without touching.

P

Pass-the-hash
Replaying a captured hash directly as authentication evidence — no password cracking needed. A replay-family attack against authentication.
Password spraying
One carefully chosen password tried against many accounts — staying under any single account's lockout threshold. Its signature indicator: widespread account lockouts.
Passwordless authentication
Signing in with passkeys, biometrics, or a device instead of a password. No shared secret exists — nothing to spray, crack, or phish. Kills password attacks at the root.
Patch management
The process behind patches: evaluate, test, schedule, deploy, verify. Automated patching buys speed at scale; manual patching buys control for the fragile few. End-of-life platforms get neither — isolate or replace.
Penetration test
Authorized attack: prove weaknesses are real by exploiting them, under signed rules of engagement. Reconnaissance, scanning, exploitation, escalation, pivoting, cleanup, report. Find versus prove — the dividing line with a vulnerability scan.
Phishing
A fraudulent lure, usually by email, engineered to harvest credentials or deliver malware. The family ladder: phishing (bulk) → spear phishing (one researched target) → whaling (executives). Siblings: smishing (SMS), vishing (voice).
Pivoting
Using a compromised host as the launchpad into networks it can reach — and the attacker, until then, could not. Segmentation quality gets proven by this, not by diagram.
Policy administrator
The zero trust enforcer: opens and closes the session based on the policy engine's decision.
Policy engine
The zero trust decider: compares each request against policy and grants or denies.
Pretexting
A manufactured story used to justify the ask. "I'm from the help desk and I need your code to close a ticket." The script behind impersonation.
Preventive control (type)
A control that stops an attack from succeeding. A firewall rule; a fence; multifactor authentication (MFA).
Private key
The secret half of an asymmetric key pair. Never leaves its owner's device. Decrypts what the public key encrypted; creates signatures the public key verifies.
Privilege escalation
Gaining rights beyond your account's own — vertical (user → admin) or horizontal (user → another user). Threat scope reduction is the zero-trust goal that contains it.
Public key
The shareable half of an asymmetric key pair. Encrypts messages only the private key can open; verifies signatures only the private key could create.
Public key infrastructure (PKI)
The full system that issues, signs, verifies, and revokes certificates: certificate authorities, registration, chains of trust, revocation lists and status protocols.

R

Race condition
Two operations race on the same resource and the result depends on timing. Time-of-check to time-of-use (TOCTOU) is the classic: the file was verified, then swapped before use. Fixed by locking and atomic operations.
RAID (redundant array of independent disks)
Level 0: striping — speed, survives nothing. Level 1: mirroring — survives one disk. Level 5: striping with parity, three or more disks — survives one. Level 6: double parity — survives any two. Level 10: stripes of mirrors — one per pair, sometimes two. RAID is availability, not a backup: it survives disk failure, not fire, theft, or ransomware.
Ransomware
Encrypts or exfiltrates data for extortion, with payment demanded under deadline and a leak site for pressure. Loud by design — an availability attack on the victim's use of their own data.
Red, blue, and purple teams
Red attacks (authorized offense), blue defends (detection and response), purple is the two in one loop — red's findings feeding blue's detections.
Real-time operating system (RTOS)
An operating system that guarantees response deadlines, not just throughput. Pacemakers, vehicle brakes, industrial actuators.
Recovery point objective (RPO)
How much data the business can afford to lose, measured in time. Sets backup or replication frequency: nightly backups mean a 24-hour RPO. The data twin of RTO.
Recovery testing
Proving the recovery plan works before the disaster. Escalating steps: tabletop (talk it), simulation (do parts), parallel (run both), full cutover (switch over).
Recovery time objective (RTO)
How much downtime the business can afford. Sets redundancy and site choice: a one-week RTO rules out paying for a hot site. The downtime twin of RPO.
Replay attack
Capturing valid traffic or credentials and re-sending them later — the server cannot tell old from new. Defeated by timestamps and nonces.
Remote wipe
Erase command delivered over the air — the whole device, or just the work container under MAM. The lost-device answer paired with full-device encryption.
Replication (storage)
A live, continuous copy at a second site — near-zero data loss. But it replicates corruption and ransomware with the same speed. A backup is a protected, independent copy; replication is not.
Resting memory reboots
Scheduled reboots that clear RAM. Fileless malware lives only in memory, so the reboot takes its home away.
Reverse proxy
Intermediary in front of servers receiving traffic in — load spreading, TLS termination, hides the backend. Serves the servers; faces the internet. An ingress controller.
RF jamming
Flooding the radio spectrum so wireless stops working at all — a physical-layer availability attack. No exploit, just noise.
Resource reuse
Cloud providers reuse disks and memory across tenants; a prior occupant's data can arrive with the hardware. A passive confidentiality leak — no attack required.
Responsible disclosure
The finder reports a flaw privately and publication waits for the vendor's fix. The ethical default; a bug bounty is the same ethics with a paycheck.
Rollback plan (backout plan)
A tested procedure for restoring the previous state if a change fails. A corrective control. The exam expects every change to carry one.
Rooting
Stripping the vendor's controls on Android devices to gain full administrative access. The Apple sibling is jailbreaking.
Rootkit
Malware at kernel or firmware level that conceals its own presence — process lists, connections, and files show nothing. Deep and persistent hiding; fileless hides in memory, transiently.
Rules of engagement
The signed document defining a penetration test's scope, targets, methods, and timeline. The line between authorized testing and crime: without it, the identical actions are an offense.

S

Safe boot
Apple's version of the signed boot chain: each boot step checks the next before running it. Secure boot's promise on macOS hardware.
Salt
A random value mixed into a password before hashing, so identical passwords produce different stored hashes and precomputed tables fail.
Scan types (discovery / port / vulnerability / compliance)
Discovery: what exists — hosts and services, the map. Port: what is open per host. Vulnerability: what is weak — versions versus known-flaw catalogs, plus misconfigurations. Compliance: does reality match the benchmark — the baseline audited, pass/fail per rule. Each answers a different question; scenarios name the question.
Sandbox
An isolated environment where code can run without touching production. A testing ground with no blast radius. A virtual machine that mirrors production.
Screened subnet
The SY0-701 name for the DMZ: public-facing services between an outer and inner filter. A breach there exposes the service — not the internal network.
Script kiddie (unskilled attacker)
Someone who runs other people's tools without understanding them — loud, opportunistic, scanning random targets. Motivation: notoriety or curiosity.
Secure access service edge (SASE)
Networking plus the security stack — filtering, inspection, access policy — delivered together from the cloud. SD-WAN is the networking half without the security stack.
Secure boot
Firmware refuses to run unsigned boot code — a boot-level rootkit is blocked, not just noticed. Block versus detect: this is the blocking half.
Secure cookies
Cookie flags that lock the session cookie down: Secure (HTTPS only, never plain HTTP) and HttpOnly (scripts cannot read it). Closes the script route to session tokens.
Secrets management
A central vault for machine credentials — API keys, database passwords — with access control, automatic rotation, and an audit trail. Dividing line: secrets authenticate machines; keys are for cryptography.
Security control
Anything an organization puts in place to reduce risk — a device, a process, a document, or a physical barrier. Every control has a category (what kind of thing it is) and a type (what job it does).
Segmentation
Dividing the network into zones that cannot reach each other freely, so one foothold spreads little. Zero trust's threat scope reduction, implemented. VLANs; microsegmentation.
Self-signed certificate
A certificate its own key vouches for — a chain of one. Fine in a lab; fails trust in a browser because no installed root stands behind it.
Serverless
Event-triggered functions on the provider's runtime — you bring code, the provider brings everything else, including patching. The responsibility ladder's top rung.
Session hijacking
Stealing an active session token and riding an already-authenticated connection. Authentication was never attacked — it was borrowed.
Shadow IT
Not an attacker — an internal risk actor: teams adopting unapproved services, creating data exposure nobody manages. Motivation: convenience.
Sideloading
Installing applications from outside the vendor's official store — bypassing the store's review. The installation act itself; jailbreaking or rooting is what strips the controls that forbid it.
Site survey
Walking the facility measuring coverage, interference, and channel overlap before and after deployment. Its security byproduct: rogue access points standing where none should be.
Snapshot
A point-in-time marker on the same storage — instant rollback, but not an independent copy. The array dies, the snapshot dies with it.
Smishing
Phishing delivered by SMS text message. "Verify your account" texts with short links.
Software self-healing
A system that detects its own failure and restores itself without a human — restart the process, replace the instance, rebuild from the image. An orchestrator that keeps restarting a sick container.
Spear phishing
A phishing email crafted for one specific, researched recipient — named, contextual, convincing. Aimed at executives it becomes whaling.
Spyware
Covertly gathers data — screens, files, conversations — and quietly ships it out. Never announces itself; the anti-ransomware. The keylogger is one tool in this category.
SQL injection
Untrusted input becomes part of a database query, so attacker text is executed as query logic. Server-side: the database is the victim. Fixed by parameterized queries — code and data travel separately.
SSL stripping
Removing encryption before the session starts — downgrading the victim's HTTPS to plain HTTP so the attacker reads traffic in the clear. Distinguish from downgrade attack: stripping happens at the HTTP layer, before negotiation.
Static code analysis (SAST)
Reading the source without running it — automated flaw-finding in the pipeline, before deployment. No running system needed; that is the dividing line with DAST.
Steganography
Hiding data inside another medium, such as a message inside a photo's pixel values. Concealment, not encryption.
Suspend to disk vs. suspend to RAM
Hibernate (to disk) writes memory to storage — the data lands at rest, so encrypt the disk. Sleep (to RAM) keeps memory powered — contents vanish on power loss, but live extraction works while it sleeps.
Symmetric encryption
Encryption with one shared secret key that both locks and unlocks. Fast — the bulk-encryption workhorse — but the key must be distributed secretly. AES.

T

Control categories
Four kinds of thing a control can be:
Managerial — instructions and decisions: policies, plans, risk assessments. Enforced through documents and management.
Operational — people running a process: log reviews, incident response, training.
Technical — a machine doing the work: firewalls, encryption, intrusion detection systems (IDS).
Physical — something you can touch: fences, locks, cameras, guard dogs.
Control types
Six jobs a control can do: preventive (stop it), deterrent (discourage trying), detective (discover it), corrective (repair it), compensating (substitute when the ideal is impossible), directive (direct behavior).
Configuration enforcement
Automatically applying and holding a known-good baseline — group policy, mobile device management, STIGs. Blunts misconfiguration and configuration drift.
Telemetry
A steady stream of device behavior and health data — the raw feed that monitoring, alerting, and logging turn into detections. Exists to shrink dwell time.
Threat intelligence
Knowledge about adversaries and their methods, from OSINT, commercial feeds, ISAC sharing, and dark-web monitoring. Raw observation until analysis turns it into prioritized action. What attackers are doing — the question CVSS cannot answer.
Test environment
A copy of production where changes are proven before they are scheduled. The step between approval and the maintenance window.
Threat actor
Anyone or anything that intends harm to a system — classified by funding, sophistication, patience, intent, and inside access. The exam describes an actor's traces and expects you to name the class and motive.
Threat scope reduction
Zero trust goal: shrink the blast radius. Segment, restrict, and use least privilege so one foothold reaches little.
Tokenization
Replacing a sensitive value with a meaningless token, with the real mapping kept in a separate, tightly guarded vault. Stored payment-card tokens.
Trojan
Malicious payload masquerading as legitimate software to get itself run. Deception instead of self-propagation — a trojan convinces; a worm travels.
Trusted operating system
An operating system built and formally evaluated to enforce separation and access guarantees — security by construction, not by configuration.
Typosquatting (URL hijacking)
Registering look-alike domains — githuh.com for github.com — to harvest mistyped traffic. The registry-package one-letter-off trick is the same move on software supply chains.

U

Unified threat management (UTM)
One appliance bundling many functions — firewall, IPS, proxy, content filtering. The small-office default. Bundling, not intelligence: the dividing line with an NGFW.
Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)
Battery power for seconds to minutes — rides through the outage until power returns or the generator starts.

V

Vendor evaluation
Vetting a vendor before purchase: breach history, patch cadence, supply-chain integrity, support lifetime. Buying near end-of-life is buying future EOL. Security decided at procurement, not after.
Virtual machine escape
A guest virtual machine breaks out of its hypervisor and reaches the host — and every sibling VM. The active break; resource reuse is the passive leak.
Virus
Malware that attaches to a host file and spreads when a user runs the carrier. Needs a host and a human — the dividing line against the worm.
Vishing
Phishing delivered by voice call — the attacker phones as IT, a bank, or support and harvests credentials live.
VPR (Vulnerability Priority Rating)
A threat-driven priority score — weighs whether exploitation is actually being observed in the wild. CVSS asks how bad the flaw could be; VPR asks whether anyone is using it.
Vulnerability remediation
The toolbox, in order of preference: patch; if no patch, segment and compensate; insure what remains; and accept only via a documented exception — named owner, justification, expiration date. Undocumented acceptance is not a control.

W

Warm site
A recovery site with equipment and connectivity in place; data restored at failover. Hours to days to operate. The middle site type.
Watering hole attack
Infecting a website a target community already visits, so the victims come to the malware. No email is sent; selection is by community — which is why it suits nation-state espionage.
Web application firewall (WAF)
Layer-7 firewall protecting one application specifically — filters SQL injection, cross-site scripting, and malformed requests at the edge, before they reach the code.
Whaling
Spear phishing aimed at executives — the CFO wire-fraud profile. Research depth and authority themes are the tells.
Worm
Malware that self-propagates over the network — no host file, no user action. Speed is its signature: whole subnets in an hour.
WPA2 vs. WPA3
Wi-Fi protection generations. WPA2: AES with a shared-key handshake that can be captured and attacked offline. WPA3: SAE (simultaneous authentication of equals) — the captured exchange cannot be cracked offline, and each session's keys are fresh. Both come in Personal (one shared passphrase) and Enterprise (per-user identity via 802.1X and RADIUS) modes.

Z

Zero-day
A flaw being exploited with no patch available — zero days of protection since discovery. Response: emergency process plus compensating controls (segmentation) until a patch ships.
Zero trust
Architecture philosophy: never trust, always verify. Network location grants nothing; every request authenticates and is authorized; design as if the attacker is already inside.
