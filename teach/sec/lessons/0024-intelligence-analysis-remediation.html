Name It, Score It, Fix It

One win today: turn a finding into a decision — score it against what it exposes, then pick the remediation that the situation actually allows. This is 4.3's second half: where intelligence comes from, how CVE and CVSS rank a flaw, and the toolbox that runs from patching to documented exceptions. The week-3 review is next.

Why this matters to your mission

Defense networks consume sector threat intelligence through government-coordinated sharing, rank findings by exposure as much as by score, and cannot simply patch an ICS controller or a weapons system — remediation there is segmentation and compensating controls with signed exceptions. The exam's hardest 4.3 questions are exactly that arithmetic.

Part 2 — Naming and scoring: the analysis half

SystemWhat it isOne-line grip
CVE — Common Vulnerabilities and ExposuresThe public catalog: every disclosed flaw has a name (CVE-YYYY-number).The name. Everyone calls the same flaw the same thing.
NVD — National Vulnerability DatabaseThe public repository that carries the scores.Where the name gets its number.
CVSS — Common Vulnerability Scoring SystemA 0–10 severity score from a standard formula: attack vector, complexity, privileges, impact.The number. 0.0 low → 9.0+ critical.
VPR — Vulnerability Priority RatingA threat-driven priority score — weighs whether exploitation is actually being seen in the wild.The number that asks "is anyone using this?" not just "how bad could it be?"

CVSS says how bad the flaw could be; VPR weighs what attackers are doing with it. Neither knows your network — that is what prioritization adds.

What moves a finding up the queue

FactorThe question
ExposureCan it be reached? An internet-facing 7.5 outranks a isolated-lab 9.8.
Asset valueWhat does it protect? Crown jewels jump the queue (Lesson 0019's classification).
PrevalenceHow widely deployed is the flawed component? One host or the whole fleet?
Risk toleranceWhat has the organization agreed it can carry? The line between "fix" and "accept."

Part 3 — The remediation toolbox

ToolWhen it fitsStatus
PatchingThe default. Evaluate, test, schedule, deploy, verify (Lesson 0020).Banked.
SegmentationNo patch, or patch impossible — shrink what the flaw can reach (Lessons 0015, 0012).Banked.
Compensating controlsThe ideal control is out of reach — substitute something that reduces the risk (Lesson 0001).Banked.
InsuranceTransfer the financial impact of what remains.New. It moves cost, not risk.
ExceptionFormal, documented acceptance — an owner, a justification, and an expiration date.New. Undocumented acceptance is not a control.

The zero-day run of the ladder

Flaw announced, no patch exists (Lesson 0012's zero-day): patch is out → segment and compensate now → patch when it ships → and if none of that is possible in time, the exception paper trail is what keeps the acceptance honest. Scan frequency rides the same logic: the faster threats move (Lesson 0009's feeds), the shorter the cadence — continuous where regulation or risk demands it.

The discrimination pairs

PairThe dividing line
CVE vs. CVSSThe name versus the number. Catalog entry versus severity score.
CVSS vs. VPRHow bad it could be versus whether attackers are using it. Potential versus activity.
OSINT vs. closed intelligenceFree and public for everyone versus paid, curated, and structured.

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from earlier lessons. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1

A finding lists "CVE-2026-10432 — CVSS 9.8." What do the identifier and the number each give you?

A name and a severity score. The CVE identifier is the public catalog's name — everyone discussing "that TLS flaw" means the same entry. The CVSS number (0–10, here critical) is the standardized severity. NVD is where the name carries its number; VPR would ask whether anyone is exploiting it in the wild.

QUESTION 2 MULTI-HOP

Before an authorized test begins, an analyst profiles the target entirely from public records, DNS entries, and job postings — the target sees nothing. Which intelligence source, and which Lesson 0023 phase?

OSINT; passive reconnaissance. Open-source intelligence is everything public — the same library the attacker reads, which is why you read it first. Active reconnaissance probes and leaves footprints; OSINT never touches the target.

QUESTION 3

Defense contractors in the same sector exchange incident signatures and attacker tradecraft hours after detection, under trusted-sharing rules that predate the internet. Which mechanism?

An information sharing and analysis center (ISAC). Sector peers sharing real attack data under trust rules — finance, health, government, defense. A bug bounty pays for flaws in a vendor's products; an ISAC shares what attackers did to victims.

QUESTION 4 MULTI-HOP

Two findings, both CVSS 8.1. One is on an internet-facing server holding customer records (Lesson 0019's classification: restricted). The other is on an isolated test host. Which factor pair correctly sends the first to the top of the queue?

Exposure and asset value. The score is identical — what differs is reach (internet-facing versus isolated) and what a compromise costs (restricted customer data versus test data). Prioritization is CVSS multiplied by your network's reality. Prevalence matters when the flawed component is fleet-wide; here the deciding facts are reach and worth.

QUESTION 5 MULTI-HOP

Lesson 0012's zero-day arrives: a flaw in an internet-facing service, actively exploited, no patch exists yet. Which pair is the exam's immediate answer?

Segmentation and compensating controls. With no patch, you shrink the reach and substitute a control — segment the service, filter the exploitation path at the IPS or WAF, watch harder. Insurance and exceptions come later for what remains; patching arrives when the vendor ships it. This is the zero-day run of the ladder.

QUESTION 6 BEST ANSWER

A flawed library has no patch and powers a revenue-critical service that cannot be turned off. Two proposals are both defensible: segment the service so only the payment path can reach it, or leave it exposed and add an IPS signature blocking the known exploit patterns. Which is better — and what is the reasoning?

Segmentation — it cuts reach regardless of variant. A signature blocks the known exploit patterns; attackers iterate past signatures (and a zero-day has no signature yet). Segmentation reduces what the flaw can reach no matter how it is exploited. The honest framing: both are compensating controls, and the strongest answer deploys both — segmentation first. Note what segmentation does not do: the flaw itself remains until patched.

QUESTION 7

A manager says "that old server stays as it is" — but formally: a written justification, a named owner, an approved risk acceptance, and a date it expires. What is this called?

A documented exception. Acceptance made honest by paperwork: owner, justification, approval, expiration. Lesson 0006's discipline applied to risk — "we'll live with it" is not a control until it is written down and revisited. Compensating controls reduce risk; exceptions record it.

QUESTION 8 MULTI-HOP

A critical CVE drops in a logging library deployed on the whole fleet — Lesson 0012's supply-chain pattern again. The organization scans annually. Which failure does the scenario expose, and what changes?

Scan frequency; scan continuously or on event. An annual scan is blind for 364 days — supply-chain flaws burn in hours, so cadence must match threat speed: continuous monitoring, event-triggered scans on new advisories, plus regulatory minimums. Credentialed accuracy and inventory truth matter, but the gap here is time: the finding arrives eleven months late.
