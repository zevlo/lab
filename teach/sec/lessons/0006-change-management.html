Change Management: Your Home Turf, the Exam's Words

One win today: translate the change process you already run into the exam's vocabulary — and defend why each step exists. New this lesson: a stopwatch. The exam allows about one minute per question, so today's quiz is timed at that pace.

Why this matters to your mission

In the DoD world, change management is not paperwork theater. Systems carry an authorization to operate (ATO) — formal permission to run. An unauthorized change can void that authorization and pull the system offline until it is re-approved. Your future DoD 8140 role will sit inside this process every week: requesting, reviewing, or approving changes. Today turns what you already do as a DevOps engineer into the vocabulary the exam — and your auditors — use.

Warm-up — recall from memory

One from accounting, one from zero trust, one from hashing. All third-and-second-pass material.

WARM-UP 1

An administrator reviews the logs of who opened the payroll folder. Which A of AAA is this?

Accounting. Recording what each identity actually did, for later review. (Lesson 0002.)

WARM-UP 2

Which zero-trust principle says to design as if attackers are already inside?

Assume breach. Design as if the attacker is already inside — one of zero trust's running principles. (Lesson 0003.)

WARM-UP 3

The random value mixed into a password before hashing is called:

A salt. It makes identical passwords produce different stored hashes, so precomputed tables fail. (Lesson 0004.)

Part 1 — Why change is a security topic

Objective 1.3 asks you to explain the importance of change management processes and the impact to security. The reasoning chain: change is the most common cause of outages; a rushed change can quietly undo a security control (a firewall rule opened "temporarily" in 2023 is still open); and an attacker who can change a system unreviewed owns it. Change management makes every modification proposed, reviewed, approved, tested, and reversible — turning uncontrolled risk into managed risk.

Part 2 — The process, in exam vocabulary

StepPlain meaningWhat it protects
1. Change requestThe formal proposal: what will change, why, the risks, and the plan to undo it.Nothing changes without a record.
2. Risk and impact analysisReviewers ask: what could this break? Who else depends on this system?Surprises surface before production does.
3. ApprovalThe change advisory board (CAB) — a cross-team group — approves, rejects, or returns the request.A second set of eyes with authority to say no.
4. TestingThe change is proven in a test environment or sandbox — isolated, production-like, no blast radius.Failure happens where it is cheap.
5. Maintenance windowA pre-agreed, low-usage time slot for implementation.Availability: disruption lands where it harms fewest.
6. Backout (rollback) planA tested procedure to restore the previous state if the change fails.A safety net — a corrective control (Lesson 0001).
7. Documentation and communicationStakeholders are told; results and deviations are recorded.The next reviewer inherits knowledge, not folklore.

Part 3 — The people

The exam names three roles. The business owner is accountable for the system the change touches. Stakeholders are everyone affected — databases, networks, applications, users — and the CAB exists to gather them in one room. Communication is the step organizations skip first and regret first: a change nobody announced looks exactly like an attack on the monitoring dashboard.

Part 4 — Emergency changes

Active exploitation does not wait for a board meeting. Every real process has an emergency change path: expedited (often verbal) approval from designated authorities, act immediately, then complete the documentation afterward. The exam's version of this: the best answer applies the emergency process now — it does not skip the process, and it does not wait. Two patterns to remember:

  • An undocumented production change is an integrity problem — a modification outside authorization.
  • A change deployed during a scheduled, announced window is business as usual; the same deployment with no announcement triggers incident response.

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from earlier lessons. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. The stopwatch starts at your first answer and stops at your last. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1 MULTI-HOP

A core switch replacement is scheduled for Sunday 02:00, when usage is lowest. Choosing that low-use moment most directly protects which triad goal?

Availability. The change still causes downtime — the window puts it where it harms the fewest users. Timing buys uptime, not secrecy or correctness.

QUESTION 2

In the change process, which step sits between approval and scheduling?

The change is tested. Approved changes are proven in a test environment before a maintenance window is booked. Writing the request comes before approval; logging results comes after implementation.

QUESTION 3 MULTI-HOP

An auditor discovers a production firewall rule that no change request covers. Which security goal did this unauthorized modification harm?

Integrity. The system state was modified outside its authorized process — the classic integrity harm, and how the exam treats undocumented changes. In a DoD setting it can also void the system's authorization to operate.

QUESTION 4

What is a change advisory board?

A cross-team group that reviews and approves significant changes. The CAB gathers the affected stakeholders so risk and impact are judged by the people who own the consequences.

QUESTION 5 MULTI-HOP

Every migration ships with a tested procedure to restore the previous database state. Which control type is that rollback procedure?

Corrective. When the change goes wrong, the rollback repairs the damage — corrective's exact job, from Lesson 0001. It does not stop the failure (preventive) or flag it (detective); it undoes it.

QUESTION 6 MULTI-HOP

Company policy: no change reaches production without approval; violations go to management review. Which control category and type is this policy?

Managerial; directive. A policy is instructions enforced through management — the managerial category — and it steers behavior rather than blocking mechanics: the directive type. Lesson 0001 wearing a change-management costume.

QUESTION 7

The platform team wants to try a new monitoring agent. First they run it on isolated virtual machines that mirror production. What is this called in exam vocabulary?

Testing in a sandbox. An isolated, production-like environment with no blast radius — where failure is cheap and instructive.

QUESTION 8 BEST ANSWER

An internet-facing server is being actively exploited. A vendor patch exists. Which action is best?

Patch now under the emergency process, documenting after. The tempting distractor is waiting for the board — process matters, but the emergency path exists exactly for active exploitation: expedited approval now, complete record after. Weeks of staging leaves the attack running; a rebuild destroys evidence and may reintroduce the flaw.
