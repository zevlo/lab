Zero Trust and Deception

One win today: map any zero-trust scenario to the right component word, and any decoy scenario to the right deception term. The exam tests these as vocabulary, relentlessly.

Why this matters to your mission

The Department of Defense has an explicit zero trust strategy — the networks a DoD 8140 role helps defend are being rebuilt around this model right now. Deception technology (honeypots, honeytokens) is part of modern network defense toolkits. This lesson is not theory; it is the target architecture of the employer.

Three from earlier lessons: one from controls, two from yesterday's CIA/AAA.

WARM-UP 1

WARM-UP 2

Part 1 — Zero trust: never trust, always verify

The old model was a castle and moat: a hard shell (the perimeter firewall) around a soft, trusted interior. Once past the moat, you could roam. That model died when laptops, cloud, and APIs made the perimeter meaningless.

Zero trust is the replacement: network location grants nothing. Every request — human or machine — must authenticate and be authorized, every time. And you design as if the attacker is already inside ("assume breach"), so access is minimal and checked continuously.

Two planes

PlanePlain meaningWho touches it
Control planeThe brain: where access decisions are made.The security infrastructure. Users never touch it.
Data planeThe traffic: where users and applications actually interact.Users and workloads — after the brain says yes.

The components the exam names

TermPlain meaning
Policy engineThe decider. Compares each request against policy; grants or denies.
Policy administratorThe enforcer. Opens and closes the session based on the engine's decision.
Adaptive identityAuthentication strength adapts to risk. New device or odd location? Step up to more proof.
Threat scope reductionShrink the blast radius. Segment, restrict, and use least privilege so one foothold reaches little.
Implicit trust zoneThe anti-pattern: an area where being inside implies trust. Zero trust exists to eliminate these.

Exam pattern

Scenario mentions deciding access → policy engine. Setting up / tearing down the session → policy administrator. Risk-based step-up authentication → adaptive identity. Limiting lateral movement or blast radius → threat scope reduction. "We trust it because it's on the internal VLAN" → the implicit trust zone zero trust removes.

Part 2 — Deception and disruption

Deception technology lures attackers onto ground you control. You learn their methods, waste their time, and get a loud alert — because nothing legitimate ever touches a decoy.

TermPlain meaning
HoneypotA decoy system, built to look valuable, that records everything an attacker does on it.
HoneynetA decoy network of honeypots.
HoneyfileA decoy document — "salaries.xlsx" — that trips an alert when opened.
Deception dataPlanted fake information (fake credentials, fake records) that only an attacker would ever use. Using it is the tell.
DNS sinkholeA disruption trick: Domain Name System (DNS) answers for known-malicious domains are pointed at a dead end, breaking malware's command-and-control (C2) callbacks.

Practice — 8 questions, instant feedback

QUESTION 1

Under zero trust, which component decides whether a request is granted?

Policy engine. It decides; the policy administrator enforces by opening the session.

QUESTION 2

The layer where users and applications actually exchange traffic is called what?

Data plane. The control plane is the brain that decides; the data plane is the traffic that flows after the decision.

QUESTION 3

A login from a new country is challenged with a stronger authentication prompt. Which concept?

Adaptive identity. Proof strength adapts to the risk of the context.

QUESTION 4

Segmentation and least privilege ensure one compromised host reaches little else. Which zero-trust goal?

Threat scope reduction. The blast radius of a single foothold is deliberately small.

QUESTION 5

A decoy server full of fake records attracts intruders and logs their every move. What is it?

Honeypot. A whole decoy system. A network of them is a honeynet.

QUESTION 6

A file named "salaries-final.xlsx" sits on a share; opening it trips an alert. What is it?

Honeyfile. The decoy is a document, not a system. No legitimate user should ever open it — so any opening is hostile.

QUESTION 7

Malware calls home to a known-bad domain, but DNS resolves it to a dead end. What broke the callback?

Sinkhole (DNS sinkhole). A disruption technique: malicious domains resolve to nowhere, so command and control fails.

QUESTION 8

An untouched honeypot suddenly shows a login. For your team, this alert is which control type?

Detective. It discovered an intrusion in progress. (Deception is detective first; its discouraging side effect is deterrent.)
