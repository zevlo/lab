Layers at the Edge, Truth in the Mail

One win today: name the control that answers each layer's failure — and prove an email is really from whom it claims. Most of 4.5 is banked: firewalls, screened subnets, baseline enforcement, monitoring. The new money is the email authentication trio (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), endpoint detection, data loss prevention, and the secure-protocol pairs.

Why this matters to your mission

Defense email is a primary attack surface: business email compromise and spear phishing arrive daily, and the mail gateway's SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks are the first filter everything passes through. The exam's 4.5 questions ask exactly what each layer contributes — and what each one cannot see.

Part 2 — Email security: proving the sender

The exam's freshest trio. Three checks, three different claims:

CheckWhat it publishes/verifiesWhat it proves — and cannot
SPF — Sender Policy FrameworkA DNS record listing which mail servers may send for the domain.The connection came from an authorized server. Says nothing about the message body or the From: display name.
DKIM — DomainKeys Identified MailA cryptographic signature on the message, verified against the sender's published public key.The message was not tampered with in transit, and it came from the domain holder. Integrity, Lesson 0004's signature, applied to mail.
DMARC — Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and ConformanceA DNS policy for what receivers should do when SPF or DKIM fails — plus reports back to the owner.The referee: aligns the other two with the visible From: domain, chooses quarantine or reject, and closes the reporting loop.

The ladder: SPF authorizes the server, DKIM signs the message, DMARC decides the policy and reports. None of them encrypt anything — mail confidentiality is TLS in transit and S/MIME end to end.

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from earlier lessons. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1

Mail arrives claiming to be from a vendor. The receiving gateway checks: did this message pass SPF and DKIM against the visible From: domain — and per the owner's published policy, what to do when they fail, with reports sent back? Which check is running?

DMARC. The referee: aligns SPF and DKIM with the visible domain, decides quarantine or reject on failure, and closes the loop with reports. SPF authorizes the server; DKIM signs the message; neither decides policy.

QUESTION 2 MULTI-HOP

Lesson 0010's business email compromise returns: a look-alike domain spoofs the CFO. Which control publishes the list of mail servers genuinely allowed to send for the real domain — catching the impostor's connection at the gateway?

SPF. The DNS record that says which servers may send for this domain — the spoofed connection comes from nowhere on the list. DKIM verifies signatures on genuine mail; S/MIME encrypts end to end. BEC loses its first step when SPF plus DMARC alignment work.

QUESTION 3

A message carries a cryptographic signature; the receiver fetches the sender's published public key and verifies it. Tampering in transit breaks it. Which control, and which Lesson 0004 idea is it?

DKIM; a digital signature. Sign with the private key, verify with the published public one — integrity and authenticity for mail, exactly the 0004 signature pattern. SPF is the server allow list; DMARC referees both.

QUESTION 4 MULTI-HOP

Malware on a laptop calls home to its command-and-control domain — but not over HTTP, over raw TCP on port 4444. Which web-filtering depth would still have stopped the callback before it left?

DNS filtering. The name never resolves — blocked for every protocol, not just web. URL, content, and proxy filtering all watch HTTP-family traffic; port 4444 never crosses them. Lesson 0013's beacon dies when its name cannot resolve (the sinkhole's standing-defense sibling).

QUESTION 5

File transfers: one option runs entirely inside an SSH session — a single connection. The other wraps classic FTP in TLS, negotiating a second channel. First and second, in order?

SFTP; FTPS. The classic pair: inside SSH (one connection) versus TLS-wrapped FTP (two channels). Your scp and rsync habits are SFTP's family.

QUESTION 6 BEST ANSWER

A novel, never-cataloged worm is moving through the fleet — no signature exists. Two endpoint products are on the shelf: antivirus with daily signature updates, and EDR with behavioral telemetry and remote isolation. Which serves better right now, and why?

EDR — behavior fires without a signature. Process ancestry, mass file changes, and propagation attempts are visible without a catalog entry — and the response action (isolate) is in the same hands. The defensible near miss: antivirus precision is real — for known malware. Against the novel, precision without a signature is zero. Note the error in option four: EDR feeds the SIEM; it does not replace it.

QUESTION 7 MULTI-HOP

An analyst tries to email a spreadsheet of restricted customer data (Lesson 0019's ladder) to a personal address. A sensor on the mail gateway recognizes the content and blocks the send. Which control, watching what?

DLP; the data leaving. Data loss prevention is content-aware: classification made enforceable at egress — mail, endpoints, storage. EDR watches behavior, DMARC watches domains, DNS filtering watches names. DLP is the only one reading the spreadsheet.

QUESTION 8 MULTI-HOP

Lesson 0014's DNS poisoning corrupted a resolver's cache — trusted names, wrong addresses. Which protocol addition makes the poisoned answer detectable, and what does it deliberately NOT provide?

DNSSEC; it skips confidentiality. Signed answers: a forged record fails verification, and the cache-poisoning trick dies. But DNSSEC signs — it does not encrypt; who-is-looking-up-what stays visible. Integrity for names, Lesson 0004's trade applied to DNS.
