The Air and the Pocket

One win today: pick the right wireless security mode for any scenario, and the right amount of mobile management — device, app, or content. This is 4.1's genuinely new half: WPA3 and SAE, personal versus enterprise authentication, site surveys, and the mobile management stack. The wireless attacks are banked from Lesson 0014 — today you learn what settings defeat them.

Why this matters to your mission

Defense facilities run classified and unclassified wireless on separate infrastructures, authenticate every wireless user by certificate, and survey for rogue access points as a standing duty — an unsanctioned AP is a reportable incident, not an inconvenience. The exam's wireless questions are that discipline in scenario form: which mode, which handshake, which management layer.

Two more MDM levers worth knowing: remote wipe (erase the device — or just the work container — on command) and geofencing (policy tied to location: the camera works everywhere except inside the building).

Part 2 — The air: encryption modes and the handshake

ProtocolGenerationThe step forward
WEP1999.Broken beyond repair — a trivia answer, never a choice.
WPA (TKIP)Stopgap.A patch for WEP on old hardware; deprecated.
WPA2 (AES, CCMP)2004.Strong encryption — but the shared-key handshake can be captured and attacked offline.
WPA3 (SAE)2018.Simultaneous authentication of equals replaces the shared-key handshake: a captured exchange cannot be cracked offline, and each session's keys are fresh (forward secrecy).

Personal versus enterprise — the mode decision

ModeWho authenticatesChoose it when
Personal (PSK)Everyone shares one pre-shared key.Home, small office, guest networks. Strength = the passphrase's secrecy; one leak re-keys the world.
Enterprise (802.1X)Each user proves individual identity — password or certificate — to a RADIUS server, via EAP.Per-user accountability, per-user revocation, central logs. Lesson 0018's port security, over the air.
Captive portalA web page asks who you are.Identity theater for guest access — acceptance, not security. Fine for the coffee shop, useless for the base.

The exam's mode question is a cost-fit question: enterprise earns its RADIUS server when identity and accounting matter; personal plus a guest SSID is the right-size answer when they do not.

Part 3 — The survey: know your air before someone else does

A site survey walks the facility measuring coverage and interference, producing a heat map of signal strength — access points placed on evidence, channels planned to avoid overlap. The survey's security half: it is also how rogue access points get found — a strong signal where no AP should be. The banked attacks, and their settings:

Banked attack (Lesson 0014)The setting that answers it
Evil twinEnterprise mode with server certificate validation — the twin cannot present the RADIUS server's certificate, so clients refuse it. (Lesson 0004's PKI, applied.)
Handshake capture + offline crackingWPA3's SAE — the captured exchange is worthless offline.
Deauthentication floodsManagement frame protection (802.11w) where supported; otherwise detection and the isolation response.
RF jammingNo protocol answers raw noise — physical location and spectrum response. (Lesson 0014.)

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from earlier lessons. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1

A cafe upgrades its guest Wi-Fi from WPA2 to WPA3. What does the new SAE handshake take off the table?

Offline dictionary attacks. Simultaneous authentication of equals replaces the shared-key handshake: capture the exchange all you like — it cannot be cracked offline later, and every session gets fresh keys (forward secrecy). Deauthentication and rogue APs remain live attacks that need other answers.

QUESTION 2 MULTI-HOP

A defense network requires every wireless user to authenticate with their own certificate, checked by a central server, with every session logged to their identity. Which mode, and which server protocol?

Enterprise mode; RADIUS. Per-user identity, per-user revocation, central accounting — 802.1X over the air (Lesson 0018's port security, wireless edition) with EAP carrying the certificate (Lesson 0004's PKI). A shared passphrase cannot log who; a portal only asks.

QUESTION 3 MULTI-HOP

Lesson 0014's evil twin appears on base: a rogue access point broadcasts the real network name, waiting for laptops to hand it their traffic. Which setting, already running from the enterprise design, defeats it?

Server certificate validation. During EAP authentication the client verifies the RADIUS server's certificate — the twin cannot present it, so no client completes authentication with it. Lesson 0004's PKI defeating Lesson 0014's attack. Encryption protects data at rest; a shared key would not help — the twin steals the session, not the file.

QUESTION 4

Staff use personal phones for work. Policy: control the work apps and work data only — the device and its owner's photos stay out of scope. Which manager fits?

Mobile application management (MAM). The work apps live in a container — managed, updated, and remotely removable — while the personal phone stays personal. MDM would claim the whole device; UEM, every endpoint. BYOD's enforcement layer is MAM, not MDM.

QUESTION 5 MULTI-HOP

A manager loses the personal phone that syncs work mail under a BYOD policy. Which pair of capabilities protects the data — unreadable to a finder, and erasable on command?

Full-device encryption; remote wipe. Encryption makes the stored data unreadable without the unlock (Lesson 0019's at-rest rule, pocket edition); remote wipe removes it — or just the work container, under MAM. Backup (the third mobile solution) recovers what the wipe removes.

QUESTION 6 BEST ANSWER

A twelve-person dental office wants Wi-Fi: staff devices and a patient guest network, one shared office passphrase, no server on site, minimal budget. Which design is the better fit?

WPA3-Personal with a guest SSID. Right-sized: strong SAE encryption, one shared passphrase for staff, guests segmented away. The defensible near miss: Enterprise — stronger per-user control, but it needs a RADIUS server and per-user certificates to protect... a waiting room. The exam rewards matching control strength to the scenario. WPS and open networks are wrong in any decade.

QUESTION 7

Before deploying Wi-Fi in a new warehouse, an engineer walks the floor measuring coverage, interference, and channel overlap, and marks every access point that appears on the spectrum. What is this called?

A site survey. Measure your own air before and after deployment — the output is a heat map of coverage, and the security byproduct is rogue access points standing where none should be. A penetration test simulates attack; the survey plans and verifies placement.

QUESTION 8 MULTI-HOP

Lesson 0013's malware crew now ships a trojan disguised as a popular game, sideloaded onto phones. Which layered pair blocks the install and contains the damage if it runs anyway?

An application allow list; containerization. The allow list blocks the install — only approved apps run (Lesson 0015's default-deny, applied by MAM). Containerization contains the blast radius — the trojan runs in the personal container and cannot reach work data (Lesson 0003's threat scope reduction). Sideloading was the vector (Lesson 0012); the store's review was bypassed, so the device policy answers.
