Threat Vectors and Attack Surfaces: How They Get In

One win today: given any scenario, name the delivery route (vector) and the entry point (surface) in exam vocabulary. Format: typed warm-ups, then timed multiple choice — half multi-hop, one best-answer.

Why this matters to your mission

Phishing remains the single most common way into any organization — including DoD's. The department literally runs permanent phishing-simulation programs: staff receive fake phishing email continuously, and clicking one queues retraining. In a DoD 8140 role you will live on both sides of that program — and the annual training you help run is a control against the human attack surface. Objective 2.2 is that daily reality in vocabulary form.

Part 1 — Two words the exam keeps straight

An attack vector is the route an attack travels — the email, the file, the phone call, the USB stick. An attack surface is the sum of every point where an attacker could get in — every open port, every application, every device, every person. Simple test: a vector is how, a surface is where. The exam loves this distinction, so lock it:

TermPlain meaningExample
Attack vectorThe delivery route of an attack.A phishing email; a poisoned update; a dropped flash drive.
Attack surfaceEverything an attacker could possibly reach and try.Internet-facing services, unpatched laptops, staff inboxes, USB ports.

Surfaces are reduced, never eliminated: close unused ports, retire legacy systems, remove local admin rights, train the staff. Every closed point is one less place to defend.

Part 3 — Influence techniques: attacking the human surface

Most vectors end at a person, so attackers aim at judgment, not just software:

TechniqueWhat it looks like
PretextingA manufactured story: "I'm from the help desk and I need your code to close a ticket."
ImpersonationPosing as a person or brand you trust — a recruiter, your bank, a vendor's domain.
Typosquatting (URL hijacking)Registering look-alike domains: githuh.com, paypa1.com.
Watering holeInfecting a site your community already visits, instead of emailing anyone.
Brand impersonationFake versions of known brands; poisoned search results steering to them.

The pressure levers never change: urgency ("act in one hour"), authority ("the CFO already approved"), and familiarity ("saw you at the conference"). Any message pulling all three levers at once is an attack until proven otherwise.

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from earlier lessons. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. The stopwatch starts at warm-up check-in or your first answer. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1

Email crafted for one specific executive references a live acquisition and spoofs a law firm's domain. Which phishing variant?

Whaling. Targeted at senior executives specifically. Spear phishing targets a specific individual at any level — whaling is the spear phish aimed up. Research plus a wire-fraud theme is the classic profile.

QUESTION 2

A text message from "your carrier" reads: "Action required — verify your account: short-link." Which vector?

Smishing. SMS is the channel — the s in smishing. Vishing is the voice-call sibling.

QUESTION 3 MULTI-HOP

Flash drives seeded in the company parking lot run malware on insertion. Which vector category, and what most directly cuts the risk of staff plugging them in?

Physical; awareness training. Hardware in hand is the physical vector, and the defense is the human one — people trained not to plug in found drives. (Vector catalog plus Lesson 0001's operational/directive controls.)

QUESTION 4 MULTI-HOP

Attackers quietly infect a small forum that power-grid engineers visit, so visitors pick up malware with no email ever sent. Technique, and who is the intended target?

Watering hole; the visitors. Poison the place the community drinks instead of chasing individuals — the visitors are the prey, the site is just the delivery surface. Selection by community is also why it suits nation-state espionage (Lesson 0008).

QUESTION 5

Users mistype the vendor portal's address and land on a look-alike domain the attacker registered months ago. Which technique?

Typosquatting (URL hijacking). Registered look-alike domains harvesting mistyped traffic — the same trick as one-letter-off package names in registries.

QUESTION 6 MULTI-HOP

A criminal impersonates the CFO over email from a look-alike domain, urging an urgent wire to a "new vendor." Which vector and motivation?

Message; financial gain. Business email compromise is a message-based vector wearing authority and urgency — and the money is the point (Lesson 0008's organized-crime signature).

QUESTION 7 MULTI-HOP

A well-funded actor compromises a vendor's update pipeline so that signed, trusted installs deliver an implant. Which vector, and which actor class fits best?

Malicious update; nation-state. The supply-chain vector: the channel is trusted, so the implant rides in signed. Compromising a pipeline takes the patience and funding of a state actor (Lesson 0008's APT pattern).

QUESTION 8 BEST ANSWER

In most organizations the largest attack surface is the humans. Which single control most directly addresses that surface?

Security awareness training. Multifactor authentication is the tempting distractor — strong, but it limits damage after credentials are phished. Training targets the surface itself: fewer clicks, fewer given-up codes. An operational, directive control (Lesson 0001).
