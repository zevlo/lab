Threat Actors: Who Attacks, and Why

One win today: read any attack scenario and name the actor class and motivation in exam vocabulary. Domain 2 is the vocabulary mountain — new names start arriving in bulk here. Format unchanged: stopwatch, half the practice multi-hop, one best-answer.

Why this matters to your mission

Your DoD 8140 role exists because of these actors. DoD networks are a primary target of nation-state actors — "the APT" is daily vocabulary in a DoD security operations center. And DoD requires formal insider-threat programs, which means someone on your future team runs them. Naming the actor from the traces a scenario leaves is the base skill of objective 2.1 — and of the job.

Warm-up — recall from memory

One rephrased rep of yesterday's review miss, its mirror fact, and one control pairing.

WARM-UP 1

You encrypt a file so that only Maria can read it. Which key encrypts it, and which key decrypts it?

Maria's public key encrypts; Maria's private key decrypts. Golden rule one: public encrypts, only the matching private key opens it — confidentiality. (This was the review's one miss: same fact, new clothes. Lesson 0004.)

WARM-UP 2

You sign a commit with your private key, and anyone can verify it came from you. Which assurances does the signature provide?

Integrity and non-repudiation. Golden rule two — the mirror of warm-up one: private signs, public verifies. Same key pair, opposite direction, different goals. (Lesson 0004.)

WARM-UP 3

Annual security-awareness training for all staff is which control category and type?

Operational; directive. People running a process, steering behavior. (Lesson 0001.)

Part 1 — The actor catalog

The exam never names the attacker outright. It describes traces — funding, sophistication, patience, intent, inside access — and you match them to a class. Learn the table as rows of tells:

ActorTells in a scenarioTypical motivation
Nation-stateBest-funded, most sophisticated; custom tools; patient; targets infrastructure, military, secrets.Espionage, warfare, disruption.
Organized crimeProfessional crews, profit-focused; ransomware, phishing, fraud; even run payment support portals.Financial gain.
HacktivistIdeology on display; defacement, leaks, disruption; skill varies widely.Ideological ("hacktivism"), sometimes grudges.
Insider (malicious)Already authenticated; legitimate access misused; triggers: grudge, payout, ideology.Revenge, espionage, sabotage, financial.
Insider (negligent)No intent to harm; phishing clicks, misdirected files, lost devices.None — that is the point.
Unskilled attacker ("script kiddie")Runs other people's tools without understanding them; loud, opportunistic, scans random targets.Notoriety, curiosity, boredom.
CompetitorTargets your intellectual property and talent specifically.Corporate espionage, advantage.
Shadow ITNot an attacker at all — an internal risk actor: teams adopting unapproved services that create unmanaged exposure.Convenience.

One boundary case worth a line: a security researcher probes systems like an attacker. What separates them is authorization — permission and responsible disclosure. The exam's motivation for this row is "ethical."

Part 2 — APT: the term you will use at work

An advanced persistent threat (APT) is not a piece of malware — it is an operating pattern, usually nation-state or state-adjacent: advanced tools and techniques, persistent access maintained quietly for months (the stretch of time an attacker stays unnoticed is called dwell time), and a threat actor with intent and funding behind it. In a DoD context, "APT" is shorthand for the adversary you assume is already patient enough to wait.

Part 3 — The insider problem

Insiders are the hardest class to stop because the failure is not at the door — they authenticate successfully every morning. The exam pairs them with exactly the controls you already know:

ControlInsider risk it cuts
Least privilegeBounds what any misuse can reach — threat scope reduction.
Accounting (the third A)Makes misuse reviewable after the fact.
Offboarding revocationKills the departed-engineer-with-a-live-token scenario — an authorization failure when skipped.

Practice — 8 questions, timed

Four marked MULTI-HOP combine frameworks from Domain 1. One marked BEST ANSWER has two defensible options — pick the better. Budget: 11 minutes for 11 questions — the exam's pace. The stopwatch starts at your first answer and stops at your last. Stopwatch: 0:00

QUESTION 1 MULTI-HOP

Over eight months, a well-funded group used custom implants to quietly map a power grid's controls and took nothing. Which actor and motivation fit best?

Nation-state; espionage. The tells: patience (months), custom tooling (funding and sophistication), and infrastructure targeting without theft — reconnaissance for espionage or future warfare. Criminals monetize; hacktivists publicize.

QUESTION 2

A professional crew runs ransomware-as-a-service, complete with a payment support portal and negotiators. Which actor class?

Organized crime. Ransomware with customer service is a business — profit is the signature. Ransomware-as-a-service is the crime syndicate's SaaS pivot.

QUESTION 3 MULTI-HOP

A departed engineer's access token stayed valid for weeks after offboarding, and proprietary data later left the network. Which actor, and which element of AAA failed?

Malicious insider; authorization. Revoking access is authorization's job — the offboarding process failed it, and a person with legitimate credentials did the rest. (Lessons 0002 and this one.)

QUESTION 4 MULTI-HOP

Activists deface a retailer's website and leak internal documents to protest its labor practices. They demand no money. Actor type and motivation?

Hacktivist; ideological. The cause is the message, the defacement is the megaphone, and the absence of any demand for payment rules out crime.

QUESTION 5

An employee forwards the payroll spreadsheet to a personal address by clicking the wrong autocomplete entry. No harm was intended. Which insider type?

Negligent insider. Intent is the dividing line inside the insider class: malicious means to harm, negligent means no intent at all — just error.

QUESTION 6

What most defines an advanced persistent threat?

It maintains long, stealthy access. The P is the defining letter — persistence, quiet dwell time measured in months. Advanced tools are common but not required; "undetectable" malware is a myth the exam does not endorse.

QUESTION 7 MULTI-HOP

A teenager runs a downloaded exploit kit against random internet addresses and shows up in intrusion detection alerts. Which actor, and which control type most often stops this class?

Unskilled attacker; preventive. Running other people's tools without understanding them — and routine defenses (patching, firewalls) stop the opportunistic. The IDS caught this one, but catching is detective; stopping is preventive. (Lessons 0001 and this one.)

QUESTION 8 BEST ANSWER

Ransomware hits a regional hospital on a holiday weekend. The crew offers a decryptor for payment and a leak-site deadline. Primary motivation?

Financial gain. Extortion is the business model; the hospital and the holiday are chosen to maximize payment pressure. Disruption is the lever, not the goal — the tempting distractor.
